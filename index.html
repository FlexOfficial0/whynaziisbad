Nazism was built on hatred, and it ended in genocide.

Nazi Germany murdered millions of people because of who they were, started a war that killed tens of millions, and destroyed the freedoms of its own citizens first. This page explains why it was wrong.

The human cost

The Holocaust was the systematic, state-sponsored murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children. Each dot below stands for 100,000 people.

60 dots = about 6 million Jewish people murdered between 1941 and 1945. Two out of every three Jews in Europe were killed.

The ideas were false and cruel

Open each one to see what the Nazis claimed and why it fails.

Racial ideology

Nazis claimed humanity is divided into superior and inferior "races" and that a "pure" German race was under threat. Modern genetics shows there is no scientific basis for this. People are far more alike than different, and the "racial science" of the Nazi era was pseudoscience designed to justify prejudice.

Even if it were true, nothing could justify stripping people of their rights or killing them for their ancestry.

Antisemitism and scapegoating

The Nazis blamed Jewish people for Germany's defeat in World War I, economic hardship and political unrest. These were lies, spread through newspapers, films, schools and speeches. Scapegoating gives frightened people a target instead of real answers, and it made violence feel acceptable.

Dictatorship and propaganda

Within months of taking power in 1933, the regime banned other parties, shut down independent unions, took control of the press and used the secret police to silence critics. Citizens lost the freedom to speak, vote, protest or disagree. Propaganda controlled what people heard, and fear controlled what they said.

Militarism and conquest

The regime believed nations should win by force and that Germany deserved "living space" taken from its neighbors. It invaded Poland in 1939 and went on to occupy much of Europe, treating the people it conquered as disposable.

Contempt for human dignity

Nazi policy treated people as valuable only if the state judged them useful. Disabled people were murdered in the "T4" program, and forced labor, medical experiments and mass killing followed. The idea that every person has equal worth and rights is the direct opposite of Nazism.

How it escalated

The genocide did not begin with camps. It grew step by step, and each step was made to seem normal.

  1. January 1933

    Adolf Hitler is appointed chancellor of Germany.

  2. March 1933

    The Enabling Act lets the government make laws without parliament, ending democracy. The first concentration camp opens at Dachau.

  3. April–May 1933

    Jewish businesses are boycotted, Jews are pushed out of public jobs, and books by Jewish and other disfavored authors are burned.

  4. September 1935

    The Nuremberg Laws strip German Jews of citizenship and ban marriage between Jews and non-Jews.

  5. November 1938

    On Kristallnacht, mobs and police destroy synagogues and Jewish shops. About 30,000 Jewish men are sent to camps.

  6. September 1939

    Germany invades Poland. World War II begins.

  7. 1941

    Mass shootings of Jewish communities begin in the occupied Soviet Union. Killing centers start operating.

  8. January 1942

    At the Wannsee Conference, officials coordinate the plan to murder Europe's Jews.

  9. May 1945

    Germany surrenders. Allied soldiers liberate the camps and find the evidence.

Warning signs worth remembering

History does not repeat exactly, but these patterns appeared before the worst happened, and they are worth recognizing anywhere.

Dehumanizing a group

Describing people as vermin, disease or threats makes cruelty easier to accept.

Blaming one group for everything

Simple scapegoats replace honest answers to hard problems.

Attacking a free press and courts

Regimes silence the institutions that could hold them to account.

Silence from bystanders

Persecution grows when most people look away. Speaking up matters early.

Survivors, historians and museums have preserved the evidence so these events are never denied or forgotten. To learn more, visit the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum or Yad Vashem.
